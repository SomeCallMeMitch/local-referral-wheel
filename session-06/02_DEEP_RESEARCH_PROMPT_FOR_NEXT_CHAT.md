## Purpose and authorization

Do not contact, message, call, solicit or represent anything to a business or owner. Do not create an outreach sequence. Do not collect personal addresses, personal phone numbers or other private information.

The founder wants small or owner-involved companies with excellent reviews whose reviews specifically demonstrate support, service, listening, communication, problem resolution and care for clients. Do not equate size or star average with quality. A large company may be less hungry; investigate, do not assume. Benicia is the starting point, not a permanent geographic limit. Include nearby firms only when their own materials and reviews show that they genuinely serve Benicia.

The founder expects to operate brief weekly partner conversations, possibly separate pair conversations when more than three partners are involved. His internal CRM tracks introductions, pending work, closed work and commissions; partners need not become regular software users. He wants low four- to five-figure monthly income from commissions rather than monthly membership fees, little urgent support, no assumed employees and potential to make an operation or system transferable. ## Review-audit method

For each serious contender, inspect the original review pages where accessible, not only search-result snippets or a company’s selected testimonials.

Use current sources such as:

- Google Business Profile/Google reviews
- Yelp
- Houzz, HomeAdvisor/Angi, WeddingWire/The Knot or other category-specific platforms
- Diamond Certified survey results where relevant
- BBB complaint, review and company-response history, remembering BBB ratings are not endorsements
- public Facebook/Nextdoor recommendations when attributable and useful
- the official company site, About/Team pages and portfolio
- Benicia Chamber/member information when useful
- California Secretary of State business status
- CSLB license status, classification, personnel and disciplinary/complaint information for contractors
- relevant public professional-license records for other regulated categories

For each company:

1. Record each platform’s current rating and review count separately. Never combine them into a made-up overall rating.
2. Read at least 25 recent substantive reviews when available, plus **all accessible 1-, 2- and 3-star reviews**. If fewer than 25 exist, read all accessible reviews and state the count.
3. Note review dates. Flag businesses whose strong score rests mainly on old reviews.
4. Detect duplicate/cross-posted reviews, provider-selected testimonials and reviews that concern a different line of business. Do not count them as independent evidence.
5. Code review evidence under: responsiveness; listening/advice; budget respect; punctuality; respect for the home; cleanliness; communication during work; problem resolution; post-sale support; repeat use; explicit referrals; owner involvement; pressure/upselling; missed appointments; unfinished work; warranty/complaint handling.
6. Include representative paraphrases and links. Use short quotations only when necessary.
7. Read the business’s responses to negative reviews. A resolved problem can be stronger evidence than a page of generic five-star praise.
8. Distinguish evidence from inference. A review cannot prove the owner wants more work, will refer reciprocally or will pay a commission.

## Business and owner verification

For finalists, verify:

- correct legal and trade business names
- years operating, apparent size and service area
- publicly identified owner or decision-maker, with source confidence
- official business website and public business contact channel
- active license/status where applicable
- whether recent reviews suggest owner involvement or delegation
- visible signs of capacity constraints, long lead times, geographic expansion or a desire for referrals
- category overlaps that could create partner conflict

Do not state that someone is the owner based only on a reviewer’s first-name mention. Do not expose personal contact details.

## Referral-network analysis

Map every plausible pair, not just the group as a whole:

- What exact customer statement or observed condition triggers the handoff?
- Which partner is the source and which receives it?
- Is the flow likely reciprocal, one-directional or episodic?
- Does the same customer commonly buy both services within 30, 90 or 365 days? Label estimates.
- What makes the introduction helpful rather than intrusive?
- Where do scopes overlap or incentives conflict?
- What happens if partners communicate directly and no longer need the founder for routine follow-up?
- What founder work remains each week, and does it fit brief separate conversations?
- Could one participant generate most of the value, and how should that affect structure?

Build one recommended three-partner configuration, one four-partner variation and one selected-pairs configuration. These are hypothetical designs, not a launch plan.

## Commission economics and the legal gate

Do not assume contractor referral fees are available. California CSLB has warned that contractor referral or kickback fees can violate Business and Professions Code §7157. CSLB also states that a registered Home Improvement Salesperson may represent multiple employers after each contractor submits the required employment notification. Research current primary sources and explain the difference among:

1. unpaid reciprocal introductions;
2. informal fees for passing leads;
3. payment for bona fide marketing/administrative services;
4. employment and sales commissions as an HIS representing one or more contractors;
5. other structures suggested by current primary law.

Do not give a definitive legal conclusion from desk research. State the exact questions that California counsel or CSLB would need to answer, including permitted activities, who can pay whom, employee/independent-contractor classification, disclosure, registration, commission calculation and whether a network operator can be associated with multiple contractors. Explain how each compliant possibility would change founder workload, independence and saleability.

For every business model, use invented ranges to show what must be true to reach $4,000, $7,500 and $10,000 in monthly **collected** commissions. Start with plausible job-value ranges only when sourced; do not infer partner margins from revenue. Show:

- suitable opportunities per month
- qualified introductions
- close rate
- average lawful collected commission
- payment lag and nonpayment
- concentration if one partner creates most opportunities
- effect of slow adoption and self-sufficient partner relationships

Do not call the income target “quick” unless the assumed lead inventory, closing time and payment time support it. A hypothetical result is not demand evidence.

Keep legal permissibility as a separate **gate**, not points that can be offset by good reviews. A high-scoring company with an uncleared payment arrangement is not commission-ready.
