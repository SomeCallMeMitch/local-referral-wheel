## Review audit

For every serious contender, inspect original review pages where accessible. Use Google, Yelp, Houzz, HomeAdvisor/Angi, Diamond Certified, BBB, Nextdoor/Facebook recommendations and category-specific sites as appropriate.

For each finalist or near-finalist:

1. Record each platform’s rating and count separately, with date checked.
2. Read at least 25 recent substantive reviews when available. If fewer exist, read all accessible substantive reviews and state how many.
3. Read **all accessible 1-, 2- and 3-star reviews**, including the company’s responses.
4. Separate recent performance from historical reputation. Flag long gaps, ownership changes or a score resting mostly on old reviews.
5. Detect duplicate reviews, syndicated reviews, provider-selected testimonials and reviews about another line of business.
6. Code evidence for responsiveness, listening, useful advice, budget respect, punctuality, communication, respect for the home, cleanliness, accuracy, issue resolution, warranty/post-sale care, repeat use, explicit referrals and owner involvement.
7. Code negative evidence for missed appointments, pressure, surprise charges, property damage, unfinished work, delays without communication, warranty resistance and failure to respond.
8. Give problem-resolution evidence extra weight. Generic “great job” reviews are weaker than detailed accounts showing how the company handled a difficult decision or corrected a problem.
9. Paraphrase representative evidence and link the original page. Use minimal quotations.

Do not calculate a fake blended rating. Score evidence quality and confidence separately.

## Business, owner and license verification

For each serious candidate, verify from public sources:

- trade name and legal business identity;
- public owner, principal or decision-maker, with the source and confidence;
- years operating and apparent company size;
- official website and public business contact channel;
- actual service area and evidence of Benicia work;
- service boundaries and likely overlap with other candidates;
- CSLB license number, status, classification, personnel, bond and workers’ compensation information where applicable;
- publicly available disciplinary actions or material complaint patterns;
- signs of owner involvement versus a review reputation attached to employees who may have left;
- signs of capacity constraints, long lead times, geographic expansion or possible appetite for well-matched work.

Do not infer that a company is “hungry” merely because it is small. Use observable clues, label the inference and state confidence. Do not name someone as owner based only on a reviewer’s first-name mention.

## Partner-fit analysis

Evaluate pairwise compatibility. For every proposed pair, explain:

- the exact customer statement, life event or condition that would trigger the introduction;
- which direction the referral flows and whether it is likely reciprocal;
- likely timing between the two purchases;
- whether the same household and budget realistically support both services;
- scope overlap, sequencing conflict or risk that one provider already performs the other’s work;
- why the introduction improves the customer’s experience;
- what the partners would handle directly after the introduction;
- what useful founder work remains each week;
- whether one partner is likely to create most of the value;
- whether the relationship can remain useful after the businesses know each other well.

Do not force equal reciprocity. A strong source partner, a strong recipient and a selected pair can be better than an artificial three-way wheel.

## Economic screen without assuming commissions

Research publicly visible project-price ranges only when credible sources exist. Do not infer profit margins from revenue or assume a percentage is legally payable.

For each category, estimate and label:

- typical project size range;
- frequency with which a provider may encounter relevant adjacent needs;
- sales-cycle and completion timing;
- whether the category has enough gross margin in principle to fund customer acquisition;
- whether suitable opportunities are likely frequent enough for $4,000, $7,500 or $10,000 in monthly founder compensation under a later lawful structure;
- concentration and seasonality risk;
- support or reputation risk if a referred provider performs poorly.

Use scenario arithmetic showing required wins at invented $250, $750 and $1,250 collected compensation points, while stating that these are thresholds rather than available fees. Do not present an informal contractor referral commission as lawful.
