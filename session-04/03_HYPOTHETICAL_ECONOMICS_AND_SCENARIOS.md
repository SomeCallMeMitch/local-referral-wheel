# Session 4 — Hypothetical economics and scenario play-throughs

2026-09-13. **Every input in this document is invented for comparison.** These are simulations, not market estimates, quoted salaries, founder targets or observed results. A favorable hypothetical outcome is not evidence of demand. No real-world test is proposed.

## Common accounting rules

USD. Founder labor opportunity cost $50/hour, replacement support labor $35/hour: analyst assumptions retained for comparability, not founder preferences. Change both in future preference-sensitive analysis. Service cash contribution = revenue less modeled operating cash costs and processing, before founder pay. Economic residual additionally deducts founder labor. It is not accounting net income; taxes, financing and unquoted insurance/legal costs are excluded. Software tables separately deduct ongoing product work, customer acquisition and a development recovery allowance.

Comparability has limits: one company, one four-payer wheel and one software subscription do not have equivalent acquisition or buyer value. Compare their mechanisms and capital/time demands, not just gross margins. Steady-state service months assume invoices collected; collection and adoption shocks are modeled separately.

## 2. Recipient and payer economics

Retain the prior recipient-first equation: available fee budget = max(0, collectible revenue − direct delivery/selling/reserve costs − required retained contribution − allocated base fee). It is a maximum affordable boundary, not the operator's entitlement. Required retained contribution covers overhead, profit and capacity opportunity cost; choosing an unrealistically low number manufactures affordability.

| Invented job | Revenue | Delivery/selling/reserve costs | Required retained contribution | Fee budget before base | 10% revenue fee | Consequence |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Small job | $1,000 | $650 | $250 | $100 | $100 | Entire modeled surplus consumed; no buffer for error |
| Large thin-margin job | $25,000 | $22,000 | $2,500 | $500 | $2,500 | Leaves $500 instead of required $2,500; size disguises poor fit |
| Recurring-service contract, first collected year | $12,000 | $7,000 | $3,500 | $1,500 | $1,200 | Numerically affordable only if receipts and delivery match assumptions |

A $300 fixed fee is too high for the small job and affordable in the other two examples before base allocation. A $100 allocated membership charge reduces budgets to $0/$400/$1,400. Billing $1,200 upfront on the recurring case would also create cash exposure if customer payments arrive monthly; affordability over a year is not monthly liquidity.

For incremental value, suppose a paying business receives four recorded wins/month, each with $400 pre-program contribution, but three would have happened anyway. Incremental contribution is only $400, not $1,600. A $250 subscription plus two participation hours at $50 leaves $50 before other costs. With two incremental wins it becomes $450. Attribution cannot determine which assumption is true.

Anchor buyer example: a $1,500 retainer saves 20 internal hours valued at $40 ($800) and avoids a hypothetical $1,000/month of economically real service failures. Value $1,800 leaves $300 before participation time. If avoided loss is only $200, value is $1,000 and the retainer does not pay for itself. Vendor profits must still be positive independently; anchor savings are not permission to underpay vendors.

Association example: a $1,200 monthly contract costs $14,400/year. If a retained membership contributes an invented $300/year after member-service costs, 48 genuinely incremental retained memberships would be needed to fund it through retention alone, before staff time. Simply reporting 48 referred members is not this evidence. Other staff savings can help but must not be counted twice.

Software buyer example: $149/month and one hour of adoption/admin time at $50 costs $199. Four useful saved hours at $50 produce $200 of capacity value, leaving $1; two hours saved leaves −$99. Avoided handoff loss may create more value, but is a separate assumption. Low entry price does not eliminate the need for customer economics.

## 3. Standalone SaaS: acquisition, retention and capital

Handoff tool baseline from document 02: $149/company/month, 3% processing, $15 variable infrastructure and 0.5 support hour at $35 gives **$112.03 contribution/customer/month**. Fixed monthly product cost = $500 nonlabor operations +40 product/maintenance hours at $50 = $2,500. This labor is separate from customer support. Initial development 600 hours at $50 +$6,000 nonlabor = $36,000 economic investment; use $1,500/month over 24 months as a recovery allowance, not an accounting depreciation claim.

Hypothetical fully loaded acquisition/onboarding cost $600 per new customer, including selling and initial configuration time. Monthly logo churn 4%; constant-price expected lifetime shortcut 1/.04=25 months. Contribution lifetime shortcut =112.03/.04=$2,800.75, giving 4.67× acquisition cost. This excludes fixed costs, time value, nonpayment and future support change; it is not a valuation. Payback is 600/112.03=5.36 months after acquisition, not from first development work. At 4% churn, about 61.3% of a starting cohort remains after 12 months.

| Customers held steady | MRR | Contribution after variable costs/support | Replacement acquisition at 4% churn | Residual after replacement, $2,500 product cost and $1,500 build recovery |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 25 | $3,725 | $2,800.75 | $600 | −$1,799.25 |
| 50 | $7,450 | $5,601.50 | $1,200 | $401.50 |
| 100 | $14,900 | $11,203 | $2,400 | $4,803 |
| 200 | $29,800 | $22,406 | $4,800 | $13,606 |

The 200-customer row is a linear sensitivity, not a staffing forecast: 100 support hours/month plus 40 product hours precede selling, onboarding and management. Support capacity cost may rise in blocks. Zero growth acquisition is assumed beyond churn replacement; growing the base requires extra spending. Under these assumptions the steady-state floor is 4,000/(112.03−.04×600)=45.44, or **46 customers**, before tax and extra unquoted costs. Increasing churn to 8% and acquisition cost to $1,200 reduces monthly net/customer to $16.03 and raises the floor to 250 customers. This is a different business risk from operating one service client.

Slow ramp: add five new customers/month, lose 4% of the existing base before additions. Month-end customers: 5, 9.8, 14.408, 18.832, 23.078, 27.155. Fractional customers are expected values. Assume full-month billing for these balances, an optimistic timing simplification. Over six months, revenue ≈$14,643 and customer contribution ≈$11,010. Acquiring 30 customers costs $18,000; product cost $15,000; initial economic build investment $36,000. Cumulative economic shortfall ≈**$57,990**. Do not also deduct the build recovery allowance here: the full initial build is already included. Much of this can be founder time rather than cash, but neither is free.

## 4. Scenario play-throughs: mechanism → economics → redesign

### Slow adoption or little referral activity

At Q4/W1, hybrid wheel revenue=$540, H=14.1, contribution=$323.80, or $22.96/hour. Success-only revenue=$450, H=14.7, contribution=$236.50, or $16.09/hour. At zero wins, the hybrid's $240 base produces $32.80 before labor; success-only loses $200 cash before labor. A minimum charge does not fund unlimited patience.

A low-volume portfolio can look profitable while a buyer still pays $1,400, but that says nothing about renewal. Recovery with almost no handoffs may be a finite cleanup job. Flat wheels need social/relationship utility worth the cost independent of immediate wins, not fabricated future ROI. Software still incurs product maintenance while acquisition is slow; use the six-month ramp above rather than jumping to 100 subscriptions.

Redesign alternatives: narrower service scope, honest finite work, availability fee only where availability itself is valued, or a tool serving existing flow. If none has adequate payer value, the hypothetical configuration is unattractive; no instruction follows to find real participants.

### One source creates most of the value

Suppose one participant generates 75% of a four-win wheel's opportunities. It may receive few leads and see no reason to pay equal dues. Free-source participation or anchor funding can recognize customer-care contribution, but the source still needs a reason to continue. If its exit removes three of four wins while fixed coordination remains, hybrid contribution falls from $1,196.80 toward $323.80 even if all $240 base remains. If its $60 base also leaves, contribution falls to $265.60. The loss is not proportional to headcount.

Redesign: different source/recipient roles, real source benefit, multiple independent sources or an anchor buying service quality. Software revenues may survive unequal referral counts if the paying user still has work; losing the dominant source can nevertheless empty its workflow and trigger churn. Do not enforce equal quotas to repair this.

### Unequal deal sizes and margins

Use the $1,000/$25,000/recurring-contract table. A common percentage can leave the biggest deal below its retained-contribution floor. Even a uniform $300 fee fails the small job. If the operator needs $300 per win but affordable budgets are $100, no contract language fixes the gap.

Redesign: lower-touch processing, anchor funding, margin-informed caps established privately, or a different service. A software subscription spreads cost over volume but can become excessive when allocated to a handful of small jobs. Outcome fees require permitted activities as well as arithmetic [E11/E12](04_EVIDENCE_AND_RESEARCH.md).

### Partners communicate directly and stop reporting

Direct communication is healthy unless a genuinely owed fee/report is hidden. In the hybrid baseline, 20% success-fee leakage reduces receipts from $1,440 to $1,200. Contribution falls to $964, $47.25/hour before extra chasing. Add four dispute hours and it is $39.51/hour. Documented source attribution is not proof every subsequent deal is eligible.

Retainer and flat-dues models tolerate direct communication if useful work remains. Handoff software should facilitate limited status updates without inserting itself into every conversation. Redesign toward named eligible opportunities, clear corrections and service fees; do not model broad long tails as guaranteed replacement revenue. If reporting costs more than the tool saves, select a product that does not require private deal values.

### Relationships become self-sufficient

Suppose a portfolio customer needs 17.6 hours/month initially but only three hours of valuable continuing work after month six. Continuing $1,400 billing is not justified by the original effort alone. A hypothetical $300 maintenance product with $100 overhead and 3% processing contributes $191, leaving $41 after three hours. It is a smaller business, not necessarily failure; if no work remains, the appropriate modeled service ends.

A $3,200 finite project can succeed by transferring the work, but at one sale every two months its $2,804 contribution averages $1,402/month before labor and idle-period overhead. Repeatable project sales are the growth engine. Software may remain useful as infrastructure after the consultant leaves; it may also churn if the mature pair now needs only email. Continuity software has better persistence when staff/partner changes recur across an organization, not for one completed handover.

### Delegating the founder's work

Retain Session 3's five-portfolio example: $7,000 revenue, $5,490 contribution after operating overhead, 96 founder hours without staffing. Assume 70% of each client's 17.6 operating hours delegates. One invented coordinator block costs $3,500 for up to 100 productive hours. Delegated hours=61.6; founder retains 26.4+8 central oversight+3 coordinator oversight=37.4h. Contribution after coordinator is $1,990; after founder time, **$120** remains. Successful delegation is not automatically a high-margin company.

If only 40% delegates, founder hours become 52.8+8+3=63.8; the same paid block leaves **−$1,200** after founder time. If owner authority cannot transfer, reminders and software do not repair it. Redesign the responsibility split, narrow workload or alter price only if payer value supports it. A transition service becomes an interesting candidate, not a proven solution. SaaS delegates relationship judgment to customer organizations but still has product/support founder dependence.

### Strong demand creates excessive work

Double recovery volume to Q40/W10: H=17, unchanged $900 retainer contributes $673, $39.59/hour, residual −$177. Required revenue merely to cover $200 overhead and $50×17 labor is $1,082.48 at 3% processing, before any extra buffer. Workload-based pricing must be accepted by a buyer whose benefit exceeds the increase; a contract cap by itself does not create value.

For event coordination, if delivery doubles from four to eight hours/event, four events require 42 total hours; $1,546 contribution leaves −$554 after labor. An anchor's expectations of emergency coverage can cause the same overrun. Redesign into bounded coordination with separately priced complex events, a defined operating-hours service, or decline the broad scope in the conceptual offer.

### Churn or anchor-buyer loss

In five equal portfolio clients, losing one removes $1,400 revenue, 20%, but the coordinator block may persist. Keeping the existing $1,300 aggregate overhead and staff for the month yields .97×5,600−1,300−3,500=**$632 before founder pay** instead of $1,990. Replacement acquisition and transition costs are extra. A sole anchor's cancellation removes 100% of its unit revenue; 20 vendors do not diversify that payer.

For software, 100 customers at 4% monthly churn require four replacements just to stay level. Losing a reseller with 50 end accounts can be worse than ordinary churn math. Redesign with independent payer groups, variable scope and capital buffers modeled explicitly. Annual prepayment changes cash timing, not long-run renewal value or customer concentration.

### Software users fail to enter data

Suppose 100 monthly handoffs are entered but only 60 have current recipient status. A screen claiming all 100 are tracked to outcome is false. Unknown 40 is the correct reporting state. If chasing each missing update takes five minutes, customer effort is 3.33h/month ($166.67 at $50) before the $149 subscription. If it previously saved four hours ($200), the combined cost now exceeds the savings by $115.67 before other admin.

Redesign: one-click limited response, fewer fields, staff-only value, selected CRM import, visible last update and unknown states. If external cooperation remains essential and absent, the tool may fail. An operator can enter data for members, but that converts the work into service labor and must appear in the combined offer's economics.

### Existing CRM is adequate

Suppose existing CRM configuration takes six hours at $50=$300 once and then one hour/month to maintain ($50). First-year incremental cost=$900. A $149/month app plus half an hour/month of administration costs $2,088/year before migration. The app needs at least $1,188/year of additional useful value beyond this CRM case. Native functionality described by HubSpot and other providers makes this a serious counterfactual, not an objection to dismiss [E1–E4](04_EVIDENCE_AND_RESEARCH.md).

Redesign: a narrower cross-company capability, a native integration, or a finite process-installation service. If existing tooling provides the same outcome, the software concept weakens even if easy to build. No managed-service prerequisite is implied by reaching that conclusion.

### Automation reduces routine work but increases errors/support

Suppose at 100 SaaS customers a new automation saves 20 total support hours but creates 30 hours of review/corrections and costs $100 in additional usage. Net monthly economic effect at $35/hour is −$450. If one additional customer cancels, lost recurring contribution is $112.03/month until replaced, with acquisition expense separately considered. Do not claim the 20 gross saved hours as net benefit.

For the AI assistant, 100 suggestions at 80% precision produce 20 irrelevant suggestions; at three minutes review each for all suggestions, review takes five hours. If only four accepted suggestions produce 30 minutes of useful saved work each, value is two hours while review is five. Redesign through selective input, higher-confidence thresholds, source citations, abstention and human approval. Any gain remains hypothetical; auto-contacting customers is not part of the concept.

### Expansion to customers, operators or territories

More local wheels duplicate relationship acquisition and add oversight; Session 3 showed per-hour returns could fall with size. A new territory adds provider coverage, travel and replacement requirements, not merely another subscription entry. Digital B2B work can expand without territory exclusivity, but time zones and customer system differences still affect support.

At 100 operator-workbench customers paying $299, one support hour each costs $3,500/month and technology $2,500, before fixed product work and selling. At 300 customers that is 300 support hours: a team, not a passive app. If four different CRMs each need 10 integration-maintenance hours/month, that is an additional $2,000 of founder time, not zero marginal cost.

The original 100 chapters×10 members×$75=$75,000/month licensing arithmetic is mathematically true but not a demand estimate. At an invented two support hours/chapter, support alone is 200 hours; operator economics must also withstand $750/month platform fees. Cheap existing group software makes that fee depend on valuable operations/brand support, not software alone. A software license can scale beyond Vallejo; a governed branded network has different obligations.

### Finite projects: repeat sales and delivery overruns

The project baseline contributes $2,804. With 28 delivery hours, it can absorb at most 28.08 acquisition hours before falling below the $50/hour benchmark, before fixed idle overhead. If selling takes 30 hours, total58h yields $48.34/hour. If delivery also grows to45h, contribution/hour falls to $37.39. Standard templates do not eliminate sponsor disagreement or staff training.

Redesign toward one purchase trigger and explicit handover deliverables, or compare a different recurring service if repeated selling is not enjoyable. This is why the earlier transition-first ranking cannot hold universally just because a project is finite and simple to describe.

## 5. Combined product plus service economics

Illustrative handoff software at $149 plus a separately priced $400 exception desk = $549/month. Allow two human operations hours at $35=$70, 0.5 software support hour=$17.50, $20 technology, $40 account overhead and 3% processing=$16.47. Contribution=$385.03. Add two founder relationship/account hours at $50 and the residual is $285.03 **before central product development and acquisition**. Software support, operations and founder relationship work are distinct tasks here.

If operations expand to ten hours, contribution falls by $280 and residual becomes $5.03. Unlimited human rescue would erase the model. Ten such customers yield $2,850.30 residual before fixed product costs; subtracting the illustrative $2,500 product budget leaves $350.30 before acquisition/build recovery. An owned platform can make a small combined service worse than using existing software. Conversely, if external SaaS customers fund shared fixed costs, never charge the full cost twice; allocate it transparently across business lines.

A license of existing software plus human operations is a valid combined business too. It lowers development exposure but adds vendor cost and dependency. A founder can specialize in relationships without claiming proprietary software is necessary. Standalone proprietary SaaS remains an independent alternative, not something it must evolve into.

## 6. What the simulations change

- One-buyer portfolio management remains a strong ongoing-role service, but five clients plus a coordinator is only marginally attractive at inherited prices.
- Transition projects lose automatic first place once repeat acquisition, idle time and founder preference enter the comparison.
- Mutual wheels remain a serious fit option. Their weakness is correlated participant value and willingness to fund continuing work, not the existence of direct relationships.
- Anchor ecosystems handle asymmetry well but exchange multi-payer friction for concentrated-buyer risk.
- Handoff SaaS can work economically without a service business, yet the startup trough and substitute threat are substantial.
- Combined software/service can preserve the connector role, provided labor is bounded and platform costs are justified; bundling does not automatically improve margins.

No simulation chooses a business. It reveals the assumptions under which each deserves more conceptual development.
