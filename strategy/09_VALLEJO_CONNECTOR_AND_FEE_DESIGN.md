# Vallejo connector concept and recipient-first fee design
Updated 2026-09-13 after the founder's latest clarification. This document controls over earlier recommendations where they conflict.

## Confirmed direction, not numerical evidence
The founder is based in Vallejo, California, is open to surrounding cities, and wants the role of **connector**. The priority is that participating businesses earn attractive profit. Fees may be smaller for larger roofing or solar deals than for smaller painting jobs, potentially using a sliding scale.

No founder-mentioned amount or percentage is a fixed rate, validated result, default target, or required input. Earlier 10%, $199 and $99 scenarios are illustrations only. Even the historic arrangement should not be treated as an audited performance record. No fee schedule, membership price or chapter structure has been approved.

## What changes strategically
E2 (ongoing partnership coordination) and E3 (one business buys partner management) now lead the founder-fit exploration. A dues circle remains a useful comparison; a council or implementation club should not quietly turn the connector into a coach or agency operator. This reorders exploration based on role fit, not proven demand.

Connector work: select compatible providers, discover what work each wants, maintain partner communication, surface customer-need triggers, facilitate permissioned introductions, check capacity and acknowledgment, resolve dropped handoffs and review whether both sides benefit. No mandatory workshops. No requirement for equal referral counts. Do not negotiate construction scope, close solar sales or manage projects merely because they came through the network.

## Profitability first: the fee has to fit a viable job
Large contract value can hide equipment, materials, subcontracting, financing costs and warranty exposure. A smaller painting job is not automatically more profitable either. The fee should be informed by the recipient's actual cost structure and acceptable acquisition cost, not a stereotype about industry margins.

Use a private pre-agreement worksheet:
- R: expected collectible eligible revenue; exclude tax, refunds and agreed pass-through amounts.
- D: direct delivery costs, including materials, labor and subcontractors.
- S: incremental selling, travel, financing/dealer and administration costs.
- U: appropriate uncertainty/warranty reserve, without double-counting costs already included.
- M: minimum retained contribution the owner needs to cover overhead, capacity/opportunity cost and desired profit.
- B: allocated membership/retainer cost, if one exists.

**Available referral-fee budget A = max(0, R − D − S − U − M − B).**

A is an affordability ceiling, not an entitlement to take the surplus and not proof of net profit. The recipient supplies/approves assumptions. M must be realistic; an arbitrary low reserve would manufacture affordability. Existing customer demand, scarce capacity and alternative uses of the crew can make a nominally profitable job unattractive.

Operator economics also matter: calculate the fee needed to cover coordination, unsuccessful opportunities, payment costs and a reasonable return. A viable interval exists only if that operator floor is no greater than the recipient's fee budget. If not, simplify work, change who pays, choose a different relationship, or pass on the arrangement. Do not solve the mismatch by squeezing the receiving business.

## Fee designs to compare
| Design | How it works | Advantage | Main problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Category-specific fixed collected-win fee | Agreed dollar amount for a defined job type and service level | Predictable, little revenue auditing | Job variation can make it unfair |
| Marginal sliding percentage | Lower rates apply only to successive portions of value | Larger jobs have a lower effective rate, without pricing cliffs | Revenue still imperfectly represents margin |
| Percentage with dollar cap | Rate applies until maximum fee reached | Limits cost on large contracts | Doesn't protect thin-margin small jobs |
| Hybrid with low base | Small amount pays for ongoing coordination; lower deal fee | Funds dry-period service | Include base in recipient profitability; no assumed amount |
| Monthly minimum credited against fees | Payer pays larger of minimum or earned fees | Avoids simply stacking dues and commissions | Minimum still costs money in months with no value |
| Private margin-informed fixed fee | Use expected job economics to agree a fee before introduction | Recipient affordability without ongoing profit audits | Needs honest cost discussion and defined exception policy |
| Profit-share | Defined share of job contribution | Can align economics | Cost disputes and data burden; not preferred starting mechanism |

Explore a margin-informed fixed/capped fee first, with a marginal sliding schedule as a comparison. The legal permissibility of any particular compensation arrangement is a separate requirement.

## Numerical illustration: not suggested market rates
To demonstrate mechanics only, suppose a schedule charges 5% on the first $5,000, 2% on the next $15,000 and 1% above $20,000, capped at $750. The schedule would produce $200 on $4,000, $510 on $18,000 and $650 on $30,000. The effective rate falls as value increases. These invented rates and amounts are not derived from the founder, researched local prices or recommendations.

| Invented example | R | D + S + U | Pre-fee contribution | M (retained minimum) | Fee budget A, no base | Sliding illustration | Maximum affordable candidate |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Painting-type job | $4,000 | $3,000 | $1,000 | $700 | $300 | $200 | $200 |
| Roofing-type job | $18,000 | $15,000 | $3,000 | $2,700 | $300 | $510 | $300 |
| Solar-type job | $30,000 | $27,300 | $2,700 | $2,600 | $100 | $650 | $100 |

"Maximum affordable candidate" is min(illustrative schedule, affordability budget). It is not a quoted fee. These intentionally different assumptions demonstrate why the largest job can support the smallest fee. If the connector cannot cover its costs within $100, that hypothetical solar arrangement is not viable. Do not impose a minimum that defeats the profitability requirement.

Adding $50 allocated base cost would reduce the three fee budgets to $250, $250 and $50. A nominally small subscription is therefore not economically invisible. If direct costs turn out worse than expected, revisit the model rather than claiming a guaranteed profit.

Avoid whole-deal bracket cliffs: charging 5% on the entire job below a threshold and 2% on the entire job above it can abruptly reduce the fee when the job becomes larger. Use marginal bands or an agreed cap and explain them plainly.

## Repeat deals and continuing coordination
Do not automatically charge the original rate on every repeat purchase. Compare:
1. Initial new job fee plus a lower defined repeat-work fee while active coordination continues.
2. Separate fixed partnership-maintenance payment, with no fee on organic repeat business.
3. A defined eligible-deal schedule during a renewable service period, with reporting and exit terms.
4. One-time partnership setup if ongoing coordination adds little.

Any continuing fee needs an agreed scope, actual continuing value, permissible structure and recipient profitability. The relationship's total lifetime revenue is not the fee base by default. A fee should not motivate unnecessary customer purchases or override who is best qualified for the job.

## California-specific operating boundary
California is now the relevant starting jurisdiction. CSLB's solar lead-generation guidance distinguishes limited referral/contact/appointment activity from selling and contracting activity. Its HIS guidance says soliciting, selling, negotiating or executing home-improvement contracts generally requires registration, with exceptions. The word "connector" does not decide how the law treats actual conduct. [CSLB solar guidance](https://www2.cslb.ca.gov/Media_Room/Industry_Bulletins/2020/January_9.aspx), [HIS registration](https://cslb.ca.gov/contractors/applicants/home_improvement_registration/before_applying_for_his.aspx).

This supports continuing exploration of a limited connector role; it does not clear a sliding referral-fee schedule. Before a live offer, review the actual provider, operator activities, licenses, payer, fee basis and relationship terms under applicable California rules. See [fee-boundary research](../research/02_REFERRAL_FEE_BOUNDARIES.md). A membership label, lower percentage or flat fee does not by itself resolve compensation restrictions.

## Most useful next exploration
Use three privately supplied, anonymized example jobs from willing providers: one smaller service, one roofing-type project and one solar-type project if those sectors remain interesting. Ask what makes the job worth accepting, their actual acquisition cost, how much coordination they want and what fee they would knowingly accept. Use hypothetical ranges if records are unavailable; never fill missing costs with the founder's anecdotal numbers.

Compare the connector's real time with each affordable fee. Choose a narrow partner-pair test only when both parties have a plausible economic benefit. No outreach, provider recruitment or fee agreement has been executed.
