## Why this deserves its own branch
The product can be the continued operation of commercial partnerships. Members do not necessarily buy meetings, coaching, lead lists or software. The operator keeps opportunities visible, aligns expectations, resolves dropped handoffs and maintains reciprocal usefulness. This may be closer to a fractional partnerships manager than a chapter leader.

The strongest economic argument is continued work that produces value for both sides. The weakest is a perpetual toll on a relationship after the operator stops doing anything useful. Evaluate the former without silently assuming the latter.

## Three materially different versions of E
| Version | Operator's job | What can trigger payment | Fit / failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| E1: Introduction desk | Originate or qualify discrete needs and route them | Defined first job from an individual introduction | Simpler scope, but fee requires continual new flow |
| E2: Ongoing relationship steward | Maintain partner pairs, review gaps, coordinate both directions and reconcile outcomes | Eligible deals from explicitly covered relationships during active service | Closest to founder's description; risks broad attribution and reporting resistance |
| E3: Outsourced partnership manager | Build/manage one firm's wider partner portfolio | Retainer, optionally an agreed performance component | Clear buyer; may be easier to price but becomes client service rather than a reciprocal club |

Keep E2 prominent in the next discussion. E1 economics in pilot/03 are a useful benchmark, not a reconstruction of the original arrangement.

## Play through a partnership
Painter and flooring firm already know one another but seldom exchange work. Operator clarifies scopes and timing, helps each recognize suitable events, makes permissioned introductions easy and reviews unanswered opportunities.

Month 1: zero closed jobs, several introductions. Who funds coordination?
Month 2: one $4,000 first job closes and pays. If 10% applies to collected revenue, fee is $400; whether that is affordable depends on actual margin.
Month 3: the same customer orders a second, unrelated $1,000 job directly. Is this eligible? The original broad description suggests it might be, but contract scope and incremental value are unresolved.
Month 4: the partners have a recurring habit and no longer use the operator. Does the agreement renew, narrow to a maintenance retainer, or end?
Month 5: a job introduced during active service pays after exit. A short defined collection tail can address this without assuming lifetime fees.

All amounts/scenarios above are hypothetical. Do not treat them as the founder's historical deals.

## Terms worth comparing, not final contracts
- Covered relationships: explicitly list pairs and start dates; identify pre-existing relationships.
- Covered deal: separate new referred prospect, repeat job from referred customer, cross-sold scope and ordinary pre-existing business.
- Attribution evidence: a relationship ID alone is insufficient; keep event/job records and a reason for eligibility.
- Continued service: define a monthly partner review, status reconciliation and corrective action; log delivery.
- Fee basis: collected revenue net of stated exclusions, fixed fee, or defined contribution share. Profit-based fees improve theoretical alignment but increase verification disputes.
- Duration: renewable 90-day relationship-management period; fees on agreed eligible activity while active. Compare first-job-only vs defined repeat-business coverage.
- Exit/tail: no assumed lifetime obligation; a proposed maximum 90-day post-exit collection tail only for documented pre-exit eligible jobs, subject to legal review and actual sales cycle.
- No retrospective claims on all relationship-derived revenue; resolve eligible scope before new work.
- Private data: only what is necessary to reconcile agreed fees, not full accounting access by default.
- Both directions: review useful contributions and outcomes without requiring equal counts/dollars or inappropriate referrals.

## Pricing alternatives and sample economic implications
1. **No base + 10% of eligible deals:** closest to reported history, but basis unresolved. Operator bears dry periods; a broad fee base may be hard to defend.
2. **$99/month + 5% capped/job:** funds coordination and lowers marginal cost; test total burden and fee coverage of repeat jobs.
3. **$99 monthly minimum credited toward earned fees:** total monthly operator revenue = max($99, eligible fee total) per payer, not $99 plus fees. May feel fairer, less stable upside.
4. **$199 flat stewardship dues:** removes transaction auditing, but successful partners may pay far less than value and dry partners more.
5. **One buyer pays $500+ retainer for its partner portfolio:** another hypothesis; easier direct accountability, less symmetry.
6. **Free source side / recipient pays:** do not charge an excellent introducer just to satisfy chapter uniformity. Payment rules and customer choice must remain transparent.

For an E2 monthly example, assume 4 partner relationships, 16 qualified opportunities, 4 first jobs collected at $3,000 plus 4 eligible repeat jobs collected at $1,000. Eligible revenue = $16,000; at 10% operator revenue $1,600. If stewardship + reconciliation takes 28 hours, gross/hour $57.14; after $150 fixed and 3% processing, $1,402/28 = $50.07/hour. At 40 hours it falls to $35.05. Repeat coverage and these volumes are unvalidated. Do not add this scenario to E1 revenue: they are alternatives.

The crucial question is not merely "will people accept 10%?" It is "will partners repeatedly verify and pay an economically tolerable fee for demonstrably useful coordination, after the relationship becomes familiar?"

## Next evidence
Reconstruct up to ten historical deals and compare first-deal vs repeat-deal receipts and effort. Ask which coordination actions caused referrals to happen or prevented failures. Interview both giver and receiver in at least three relationships. Price the actual ongoing work, then compare with a retainer/dues alternative.

Do not reject E2 because membership is simpler. Do not prefer E2 solely because revenue can scale with deals. Its continuity, margins, collection behavior and founder enjoyment determine whether it is attractive.
