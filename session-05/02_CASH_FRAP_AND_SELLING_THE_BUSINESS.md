## Three concrete things you might mean by “sell it and move to the next”

### A. Sell an operating referral network or portfolio

You create and operate a small network, then sell the business or a transferable portfolio to another capable operator. The buyer takes over the weekly conversations, tracking and commission administration. You move to another project, possibly after a finite transition period. This is the interpretation most consistent with wanting to build something and then move on, but it is not yet confirmed.

**What the buyer is actually buying:** potentially transferable commission agreements; permissioned operating records; an understandable pattern of deal flow; partner acceptance of the successor; reputation/brand rights where included; and a system the buyer can operate. Partners and clients are not owned assets, and continuing voluntary referrals cannot be guaranteed by the sale.

**Why someone could pay:** buying a functioning operation could be more useful than starting relationships and systems from scratch. A prospective buyer might be an independent connector or an adjacent business, but the latter can change network neutrality. No actual buyer demand or transferable agreement rights are established.

**What determines value:** earnings after replacing your work, concentration, repeatable source activity, reporting reliability, eligible fee duration and whether partners stay after you leave. Your past ability to earn a large commission does not establish a transferable business. Selling an operating service whose work vanishes with the seller is much harder than selling a system with durable customer agreements.

**Invented buyer arithmetic, not a valuation:** $5,000/month collected commissions minus $250 operating cash costs minus 25 hours of replacement work at $40 leaves $3,750/month before buyer tax and additional costs. An arbitrary $22,500 purchase price would equal six months of that contribution. If commissions fall 50% after transfer, contribution is $1,250 and the same price takes 18 months to recover. If there are no continuing eligible commissions, there is no operating-earnings payback. These examples illustrate price sensitivity; no market multiple is asserted.

**Your tradeoff:** a clean cash sale limits future upside but helps you move on. An earn-out ties part of payment to later performance, sharing transfer risk but keeping you exposed to reporting and buyer conduct. Retaining a portion of future commissions may make the buyer's economics worse. Do not add full sale proceeds and all future fee income as if both can be sold and retained simultaneously.

This option is directly worth further conceptual development. It is not the same as selling a one-time transition project to a provider.

### B. Sell or license a repeatable operator system

Instead of selling a particular local network, you sell other connectors an original playbook, a tracking/reporting system, training and a defined support package. Each buyer builds/operates its own relationships and receives commissions. You own the system business and need not personally manage every local relationship.

**An actual hypothetical package:** partner-fit worksheets and example trigger maps; a brief weekly conversation process; private pending/won/paid reporting; optional lead form; eligibility/reconciliation workflow for arrangements independently reviewed as appropriate; transition guidance; tool access and a bounded onboarding session. This is a description of a possible product, not materials prepared for launch or a claim that templates grant legal permission.

**How you earn:** outright package sale, fixed software/license charge to the operator, or a share of the operator's collected commissions. The last can align with your commission preference without imposing member dues on participating businesses. These are alternative payment structures to compare, not selected terms.

**Illustration:** ten operators each collect $6,000/month, with an invented 10% system royalty → $6,000/month gross to the system owner. Each operator retains $5,400 before its own expenses. Your royalty is deducted from their receipts; it is not additional value created by double-counting the same commission. If operators instead average $1,500, your gross is $1,500. A fixed $3,000 package sale to two operators/month also gives $6,000 gross, but requires repeat sales and delivery; these are separate cases.

**The work that remains:** selling the package, onboarding operators, maintaining software and answering questions. At two support hours/operator/month, ten operators need 20 hours before development and selling; fifty need 100. Bound scope in the concept, but do not simply assume buyers will never need help. Using independently owned operators reduces employee management while creating business-customer support responsibilities.

**Why FRAP is relevant:** it illustrates packaging and distribution through leaders. It does not prove that a commission-network system will command the same price or that royalties will be collectible. This can be explored conceptually now; no requirement to conduct a real-world network pilot first.

**Commercial boundary:** a software sale, a method license and a branded operating network are not interchangeable. Actual brand/control/support/payment arrangements determine what further franchise or business-opportunity analysis may be needed; this research does not classify your proposed model or FRAP legally. The FTC describes how branded systems can combine fees, operating formats, support and contractual controls. [FTC franchise guide](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/consumers-guide-buying-franchise).

### C. Sell a completed partnership setup to the participating businesses

A company pays you for a finite assignment: structure one or several partnerships, establish ownership and an introduction/status process, then hand it over. Its staff carries on, and you sell the next engagement. This is a professional service, not the sale of your operating business.

**Why it might fit:** finite delivery and no employee base. **Why it might not:** the income comes from project fees unless an independently justified commission arrangement is included, and it does not preserve the ongoing weekly connector role after handover. Your desire for commission income means this should not quietly become the default again.

**Illustration:** two $3,000 projects/month yield $6,000 gross before costs and work. That does not establish a reliable two-project sales cadence. It also does not mean you sold a $6,000/month recurring asset; you performed two jobs. This interpretation ranks behind A/B for current development unless you clarify otherwise.
